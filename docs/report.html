Data center load on the U.S. grid

Evidence from hourly demand in PJM zones, 2019 to the present, and its carbon cost at the margin. Technical report.

Abstract. Has the build-out of data centers since 2023 left a measurable mark on U.S. electricity demand, and what does serving it emit? I use million hourly records from EIA Form 930 covering balancing authorities and PJM load zones. Two zones separate from the rest. In the AEP zone, demand growth moved from % to % a year; a synthetic control that tracked the zone within % before 2023 is now % below the zone, a gap of MW that ranks zones in placebo tests. The Dominion zone is % above its control, but that divergence predates 2023; the speed-up beyond its earlier trend is MW and is not statistically conclusive. Structural break tests date the change to in AEP and in Dominion. In PJM, one more MWh of demand adds t of CO₂, % more than the average MWh. Serving the load that is new since 2023 emits about Mt of CO₂ a year. Demand is observed by zone, not by customer, so attribution to data centers is circumstantial.

1. Question

Utilities and grid operators have reported sharp increases in requests to connect large data centers, and forecasts of U.S. electricity demand have been revised upward after more than a decade of flat load. Forecasts and interconnection queues describe intentions. This report asks what has already happened in metered demand, using only public data, and puts three questions to it.

  • Where, and by how much, has demand departed from its earlier path?
  • When did the departure begin?
  • What does the additional demand emit, given which plants respond to it?

PJM is the natural place to look. It is the largest U.S. grid operator, it contains Northern Virginia, the largest data center market in the world, and it publishes hourly demand for each of its utility zones. Zones inside one market share weather systems, fuel prices and a regional economy, which makes them usable as comparisons for one another.

2. Data

2.1 Source

All data comes from the U.S. Energy Information Administration’s Form EIA-930, the Hourly Electric Grid Monitor, downloaded as six-month bulk files. Two tables are used: the balance table (hourly demand, net generation and generation by energy source for each balancing authority) and the subregion table (hourly demand for zones within the larger authorities). The sample runs from to UTC. Ten balancing authorities are kept: PJM, ERCOT, MISO, SPP, NYISO, ISO-NE, CAISO, Southern Company, TVA and Duke Energy Carolinas. EIA’s anomaly-adjusted columns are used where they exist.

2.2 Cleaning

The feed contains occasional reporting errors, including zeros and values several orders of magnitude too large. Hourly demand is cleaned in three steps. Values at or below zero are removed. Values more than 2.5 times, or less than 0.4 times, the median of the surrounding week (a centered 169-hour window) are removed. Gaps of up to six hours are filled by linear interpolation. A month is used only if at least 90% of its hours are present. In PJM zones the filter removes well under 0.01% of hours, except in the small Rockland zone, which is dropped for incomplete coverage. That leaves zones.

2.3 Emissions

EIA-930 does not report emissions. Hourly CO₂ for each region is estimated as generation from coal, natural gas and petroleum multiplied by a fuel-specific factor, taken from EIA’s 2023 national totals of emissions and generation: t/MWh for coal, for gas and for petroleum. All other sources are counted as zero. Section 6 discusses what this misses.

3. Methods

3.1 Change in growth

For each zone and region, let yt be monthly mean demand (or the monthly 5th-percentile hour, as a measure of baseload). I estimate

log yt = αm(t) + β1 τt + β2 max(τt, 0) + εt

where αm are month-of-year effects and τt is time in years measured from January 2023. Growth is exp(β1) − 1 before and exp(β1 + β2) − 1 after; the test of β2 = 0 asks whether growth changed. Standard errors are Newey-West with six lags. January 2023 is the first full month after the public release of ChatGPT and is fixed in advance for every unit.

3.2 Synthetic control

For a zone of interest, the outcome is log monthly demand measured against the zone’s own 2019–22 mean, which removes level differences between zones. Weights w over the J comparison zones solve

minw Σt < 2023 ( y1t − Σj wj yjt )2   subject to   wj ≥ 0,   Σj wj = 1

and the estimated effect in month t is the gap between the zone and its weighted comparison. Because the comparison zones experience the same weather systems, no temperature model is needed. I report the average gap over the latest 12 months, converted to megawatts.

Two versions are estimated. The levels version is as above. The acceleration version first removes each zone’s own pre-2023 linear trend, extended forward, so the gap measures only growth beyond what the zone was already doing. The second is the appropriate estimand when a zone was diverging before the period of interest.

Inference follows Abadie, Diamond and Hainmueller (2010). Each comparison zone is treated in turn as if it were the zone of interest, and the ratio of root mean squared prediction error after 2023 to before is computed for all. The p-value is the rank of the true zone’s ratio. With comparison zones the smallest attainable p-value is . I also refit leaving out each comparison zone that carries weight, and run a placebo in time that uses only pre-2023 data and pretends the build-out began in January 2021.

Dominion was designated as the zone of interest before any estimation. AEP was not. It emerged as the fastest-accelerating zone in the comparison of Section 3.1, and is reported as a second zone of interest with that caveat. Each is excluded from the other’s comparison pool.

3.3 Dating the change

Each zone’s share of total PJM demand cancels weather that is common across zones. To date a change in its trend I regress the monthly share on month effects and a linear trend, add a term max(t − k, 0) for a kink at month k, and take the largest F statistic over all k in the middle 70% of the sample (Andrews, 1993). The p-value is from a moving-block bootstrap (Künsch, 1989) of the residuals of the no-break model, with blocks of six months and 999 draws, which preserves serial correlation.

3.4 Marginal emissions

Following Hawkes (2010) and Siler-Evans, Azevedo and Morgan (2012), the marginal emission factor is the slope in a regression of hourly changes:

ΔCO2,t = a + b ΔDt + c1 ΔWt + c2 ΔSt + ut

where D is demand, W wind output and S solar output. Wind and solar do not respond to demand, but they ramp at the hours demand does; without the controls, a solar-rich region’s morning ramp is mistaken for a clean response to load. Hours where either difference lies more than eight robust standard deviations from its median are dropped. Standard errors are heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent with 24 lags. The model is estimated by region and year, and pooled with an interaction to test for a change after 2023. Replacing the dependent variable with the hourly change in each source’s output gives the share of each source in the response to one more MWh.

5. Robustness

  • Comparison pool. Leave-one-out refits move the AEP estimate between and MW. No single comparison zone drives it.
  • Pre-existing trends. The placebo start date separates the two zones cleanly: % for AEP and % for Dominion. This is why Dominion’s headline number uses the acceleration version.
  • Contamination between zones. Each zone of interest is removed from the other’s comparison pool. Other zones may also host data centers, which would bias the estimates toward zero.
  • Load shape. Load factor has risen in both zones and fallen in the rest of PJM, consistent with added round-the-clock load. Baseload, the 5th-percentile hour, shows the same acceleration as mean demand.
  • Estimator checks. On a synthetic grid with a planted 10% a year effect and a planted marginal factor, the code recovers both within tolerance and finds nothing in the zones with no planted effect. These tests run before every weekly refresh.

6. Limitations

  • Attribution. EIA-930 reports demand by zone. Factories, electrification and population growth also add load. The case for data centers rests on location, timing, the flatness of the added load and public reporting, not on direct observation.
  • Selection of AEP. AEP was identified from the data. Its p-value does not account for having searched across zones for the largest change.
  • Coarse inference. Placebo p-values cannot fall below with this many zones.
  • Emission factors. One national factor per fuel ignores differences in plant efficiency. Marginal gas units are often less efficient than the fleet average, so the marginal factor here is more likely too low than too high.
  • Regional boundary. Emissions are counted within each balancing authority. Imports, about of PJM’s marginal response, are not traced to their source.
  • Short-run margin. The marginal factor describes which existing plants respond hour to hour. It does not capture new plants built, or retirements delayed, because of sustained load growth.
  • Behind-the-meter supply. Data centers with on-site generation do not appear in zonal demand.

7. Reproducibility

The pipeline is three Python scripts: a downloader, the analysis and a test suite. It needs no API key and runs in about two minutes on a standard GitHub Actions runner, which re-executes it every Monday and republishes the results file this report reads. Random seeds are fixed. The repository contains the exact code that produced every number here.
