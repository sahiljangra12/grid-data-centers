Data centers are bending the grid

Two regions of America’s largest power grid have broken away from the rest. This study measures by how much, using every hour of public grid data since 2019, and what the extra demand costs in carbon.

See the findings

Two zones pulled away

PJM, the grid that runs from Chicago to Virginia, reports hourly demand for each of its utility zones. For every zone, a regression compares yearly growth before 2023 with growth since, after removing the seasonal cycle.

Dominion’s zone covers Northern Virginia, the world’s largest data center market, and was chosen in advance. The AEP zone, which includes central Ohio, was not: it surfaced from this comparison. Trade reporting identifies Ohio as the newer data center hub on PJM’s system.

Demand data cannot see what is behind a meter. The pattern fits data centers in timing, place and shape, but this study does not observe them directly.

Measured against a statistical twin

A synthetic control builds a stand-in for each zone: a weighted mix of other PJM zones, with weights chosen so the mix reproduces the zone’s demand before 2023. The same weather systems and economy move all of them, so what is left after 2023 is what is particular to the zone.

To judge whether a gap is unusual, the same procedure is run on every other zone as if it were the one under study. The grey band shows where 90% of those placebo gaps fall.

When the bend came

Each zone’s share of total PJM demand cancels out most weather, because a heat wave lifts every zone at once. A sup-F test searches every possible month for a change in the trend’s slope; a block bootstrap gives the p-value.

Not every grid is feeling it

Baseload here is the 5th-percentile hour of each month: the demand that is there at 4 a.m. in mild weather. Round-the-clock loads such as data centers raise it directly, and weather moves it less than it moves peaks.

Filled markers are changes that are statistically significant at the 5% level, using Newey-West standard errors. Other things also raise baseload, including new factories, crypto mining and electrification.

The next megawatt-hour is dirtier than the average one

Nuclear plants run flat out regardless of demand. When demand rises, the plants that respond are mostly gas and coal. The marginal emission factor captures this: it is the slope of hourly changes in CO₂ on hourly changes in demand, holding wind and solar output fixed.

Carbon accounts that multiply new load by the grid’s average intensity miss that difference.

Method and limits

Data

Models

  • Growth change: log demand on month effects and a trend that may bend in January 2023; Newey-West errors.
  • Synthetic control: non-negative weights summing to one; inference by placebo zones, leave-one-out refits and a placebo start date.
  • Break date: sup-F over the middle 70% of months; moving-block bootstrap, 999 draws.
  • Marginal emissions: hourly first differences with wind and solar controls; HAC errors, 24 lags.

What this cannot show

  • Demand is by zone, not by customer. Attribution to data centers rests on timing, location and load shape.
  • CO₂ uses one national factor per fuel, so it misses differences between efficient and inefficient plants.
  • Emissions are counted inside each region; power imported from neighbors is not traced to its source.
  • No temperature data is used. Zones are compared with neighbors under the same weather instead.
